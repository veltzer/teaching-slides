# Retrospectives

---
## What This Chapter Covers

- Why the Retrospective is the most important Scrum event
- Common formats
- How to make actions stick
- Creating psychological safety
- Anti-patterns to avoid
- Distributed and remote retros

---
## Why Retrospectives Matter

- Every other Scrum event is about the *product*
- The Retrospective is about the *team*
- Without it, teams stop improving
- A team that keeps doing what it did last Sprint will keep getting what it got
- Skipping the Retro is the most expensive saving in Scrum

## Time-Box and Attendees

- Up to 3 hours for a 4-week Sprint, proportionally less for shorter
- The whole Scrum Team — Developers, Scrum Master, Product Owner
- No external observers unless explicitly invited
- Held immediately after the Sprint Review, before the next Sprint Planning
- Same time, same cadence — the rhythm is part of the discipline

---
## The Three Questions

- What went well?
- What didn't go well?
- What should we change?
- Simple, durable, gets useful answers
- Many teams use this without ceremony — it works

---
## Format: Start / Stop / Continue

- What should we **start** doing?
- What should we **stop** doing?
- What should we **continue** doing?
- Forces concrete actions, not just observations
- Pairs well with sticky notes on a wall (or a virtual board)

---
## Format: Mad / Sad / Glad

- What made you **mad** this Sprint?
- What made you **sad**?
- What made you **glad**?
- Surfaces emotional content — useful for teams that suppress it
- Less suitable for teams uncomfortable with emotional vocabulary

---
## Format: 4Ls

- **Liked**: what worked
- **Learned**: what we now know that we didn't
- **Lacked**: what was missing
- **Longed for**: what we wished we had
- Good for teams new to retros — gentler entry than "what went wrong"

---
## Vary the Format

- Same format every Sprint &#8594; people game it, give shallow answers
- Rotate through 3-4 formats
- Try one new format per quarter
- The Scrum Master usually picks; sometimes a developer leads instead
- Variety keeps the conversation alive

---
## From Discussion to Action

- A Retro that produces only complaints is wasted
- End with 1-3 *concrete* actions: who, what, by when
- Track them: review last Retro's actions at the start of the next one
- If the same item shows up Retro after Retro and never gets done, escalate
- Three actions taken beats fifteen written down and forgotten

---
## Psychological Safety

- People won't share real problems if they fear consequences
- Ground rules: no blame, focus on systems not individuals
- Manager presence usually reduces safety — discuss before inviting
- The Scrum Master watches for body language and silence
- A safe Retro is the foundation of a useful Retro

---
## The Vegas Rule

- "What's said in the Retro stays in the Retro"
- *Decisions* and *actions* are public; the discussion that led to them isn't
- Without this, people self-censor
- Violating it once destroys it forever
- Worth restating at the start of every Retro

---
## Common Anti-Patterns

- "Same as last time" answers — format has gone stale
- One person dominates and others stay quiet
- The team identifies an action that requires management buy-in, then nothing happens
- Discussion of one specific person's behaviour by name in front of everyone
- Skipping the Retro because "we're behind on the Sprint"

---
## Remote Retrospectives

- A virtual board (Miro, Mural, EasyRetro) is the workhorse
- Start with silent writing — everyone adds notes for 5 minutes
- Then group, then discuss, then vote on what to act on
- Cameras on if possible; this is one event where presence really helps
- Time-box discussion of each topic — virtual silences are heavier than in-person ones

---
## Measuring Retrospectives

- Count completed actions over the last 4 Retros
- Track repeat themes — they hint at deeper issues you keep deferring
- Anonymous health survey once a quarter: "Do you find the Retro valuable?"
- Adjust the format based on what you find
- A Retro about the Retros, occasionally, is healthy
