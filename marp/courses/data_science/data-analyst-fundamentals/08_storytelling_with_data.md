# Storytelling with Data

---
## What This Chapter Covers

- Why storytelling matters for analysts
- The structure of a data narrative
- Audience awareness
- Designing for clarity and impact
- Common mistakes in data communication
- Templates for common formats

---
## Why Storytelling Matters

- Analysis without communication is decoration
- Stakeholders make decisions on the *story*, not the data
- A well-told story with mediocre data beats a brilliant analysis told badly
- This is the most undervalued analyst skill
- Time spent here pays off more than another tool to learn

---
## The Three Audiences

- **Decision-makers**: want the recommendation up front
- **Domain experts**: want the methodology and assumptions
- **Other analysts**: want the data and the code
- Most analyses serve all three; structure for each
- A good report has layers — surface to depth

---
## The Structure of a Data Narrative

- **Setup**: what's the situation?
- **Conflict**: what changed, what's at stake, what's the question?
- **Resolution**: what did the data say?
- **Action**: what should we do?
- This is just storytelling, applied to a memo

---
## Lead With the Bottom Line

- Decision-makers read the first paragraph and the last
- "We should fund Project A; here's why" — paragraph 1
- Methodology and detail follow
- Burying the lead is the most common mistake
- The Pyramid Principle (Barbara Minto) is the canonical reference

---
## Match Tone to Audience

- Executives: short, decisive, confident
- Engineering: precise, methodical, includes the gotchas
- Marketing: vivid, customer-language, focuses on impact
- Same data, different telling
- One report rarely fits all audiences

---
## Designing for Clarity

- One chart, one message
- Title the chart with *the takeaway*, not the metric
- "Sales rose 15% in Q4" beats "Quarterly Sales"
- Highlight the data point that matters; grey out the rest
- Use color to draw the eye, not as decoration

---
## The Annotation Layer

- Numbers without context are noise
- Add: callouts on key points, baseline comparisons, target lines
- "Above target" or "below baseline" beats raw numbers
- One key annotation does more than a paragraph of explanation
- Label sparingly; one or two callouts per chart

---
## The Scale Question

- Linear y-axis: differences in absolute terms
- Log y-axis: relative changes; useful for huge ranges
- Index to 100 at a baseline date for relative comparison
- Don't switch scales mid-series; you'll mislead
- Always label the axis clearly

---
## Choosing What to Show

- Less is more: every chart is a cost on the reader
- Cut everything that doesn't serve the takeaway
- Lead with the most important; bury the supporting evidence in an appendix
- Edit ruthlessly — your reader will thank you
- "I didn't have time to write a short report, so I wrote a long one" — Twain

---
## Designing Slides

- One slide, one message — same as one chart, one message
- Title is the takeaway
- Body supports it
- Avoid bullet vomit; use the speaker (you) for context
- 2-3 slides for executive summaries; deep details in an appendix

---
## Designing Reports

- Executive summary at the top: 3-5 bullets
- Sections with clear headings
- Charts inline with the text that interprets them
- Methodology and data sources at the end
- Searchable, skimmable, navigable

---
## Designing Dashboards

- Most-important metric at top-left
- Group related metrics together
- 5-7 charts, not 20
- Clear date / segment filters at the top
- Mobile-friendly if it'll be viewed on phones

---
## The "So What" Test

- For every chart and number: ask "so what?"
- "Sales rose 15%" — so what? "We should reinvest in the channel that drove it."
- A finding without an action is incomplete
- Train yourself to always include the action
- Better questions get better answers

---
## Common Mistakes

- Recommending nothing — leaving the decision-maker to draw conclusions
- 50-page reports nobody reads
- Charts without titles, axes, units
- "Interesting" findings that don't suggest action
- Overconfidence: presenting uncertain results as certain

---
## A Template

- **Question**: what we set out to learn
- **Answer**: the headline finding
- **Recommendation**: what we should do
- **Method**: how we got there (briefly)
- **Caveats**: what we don't know
- **Appendix**: charts, data, code

---
## Storytelling Resources

- Stephen Few — *Show Me the Numbers* (dashboards)
- Cole Nussbaumer Knaflic — *Storytelling with Data* (general)
- Edward Tufte — anything (visual design)
- Barbara Minto — *The Pyramid Principle* (executive writing)
- Read the books once, internalise the principles forever
